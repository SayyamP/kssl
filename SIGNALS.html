What we are actually trying to make

A signal is a short, dated statement that tells KSSL to do something, and shows its working. Not a summary of an article — a reason to act, with a source.

“The platform India just committed to sustaining is the one the US Army has started replacing, and the replacement contract went to a competitor who is also bidding in India.”

That sentence is worth money. It is also impossible to produce from any single article, which is the entire problem this page is about.

Where we are today

Right now a signal is made by handing one article to a language model and asking it what matters. It works, and we have spent weeks tightening it: gates for dates, for duplicates, for relevance, and a separate check for every field the model writes, because any field without a check is where the invented facts collect.

But the ceiling is structural, not a matter of prompt quality. A reader of one article can never see a pattern that lives across many. It cannot notice that the same rival has appeared four times in six weeks. It cannot notice that a buyer is shopping and we are not on the list, because absence is never written down in an article. And it has to be re-run from scratch every time anything changes, because it remembers nothing.

The rest of this page is one argument: we already own the two things that fix this, and they have simply never been connected to each other.

The two things we already own

A corpus, broken down

650 documents in 26 languages, pulled apart into 220,014 spans and 18,673 statements of the form subject — verb — object, each one carrying the exact character range of the sentence it came from.

An ontology

Version 0.2.0. A deliberately small tree of what defence things are, 62 sector nodes deep where the sector is genuinely understood, with 33 legal link types, 5 facets and 12 measurable properties — each node named in 26 languages.

The corpus knows what was said. The ontology knows what things are. Between them sits everything KSSL wants to know — and there is currently no join between them at all.

The four joins that are missing

Connecting the corpus to the ontology is four separate pieces of work. Each one is small. Each one is currently at zero.

J1  —  Type the thing

extracted.entity.ont_node_id

Every entity gets told what KIND of thing it is, using the ontology's own node ids — so K9 Vajra stops being a string and becomes materiel.weapons.artillery.

The corpus already contains its own answer key. The single commonest thing our extractor writes is an is a statement — 2,445 of them — and an is a statement is a typing statement: “the Mobile Tactical Cannon is a self-propelled howitzer”. We match those objects, and the entity's own spellings, against the node labels. The labels are written in 26 languages precisely because English-only node descriptions measured 20–25% failure on German, Japanese, Chinese and Korean sources against 2.8% on English. Whatever still does not match goes to the unclassified slot and is counted. We never force a fit; residue is a number we report, not a failure we hide.

J2  —  Type the link

a closed predicate on every proposition

The 5,409 different phrasings our extractor produced collapse onto the ontology's 33 legal link types — manufactures, awarded, procures, replaces, and the rest.

This is the join that pays for itself twice. First, an open set of 5,409 relations is not a graph — nothing can be queried across it, because “won”, “secured” and “captures” are three different edges to a database and one idea to a reader. Second, and better: each of the 33 link types declares what may sit at each end. manufactures runs from an Agent to an Artefact. So once J1 has typed both ends, “Saab manufactures Gripen” is accepted and “Gripen manufactures Saab” is rejected by arithmetic — no model, no reviewer, no prompt. We have been repairing backwards facts by hand; this makes most of them impossible to store.

J3  —  Type the number

extracted.span_value, extracted.prop_arg

Dates, calibres, weights, money and quantities stop being text and become values with units, so two of them can be compared.

We have found 13,096 measurable spans and parsed exactly none of them. That single fact explains three empty screens: there is no “₹ at stake” because contract values are strings; no deadline sorting because dates are strings; no spec comparison because 155 mm is a string. The property registry also carries the guard that makes comparison legal — every property declares a dimension, so a 500 mm calibre can never be compared against 500 mm of armour, which is the classic way a spec table quietly lies.

J4  —  Clean the identity

extracted.entity, entity_alias

Merge the spellings of one company into one company, and throw out the things that are not companies at all.

This one is not theory. Rank our entities by how often they are mentioned and 4 of the top 12 are cookie, website, cookies, Company — consent banners and boilerplate outranking real firms. In the same top 12, BrahMos, BrahMos Aerospace, BrahMos pavilion sit as separate things when they are one company and one of its trade-show stands. Until this is fixed, every count on every screen is a count of spellings, not of companies.

Order matters. J1 must run before J2, because the domain-and-range check needs both ends of a link to have a type before it can tell a legal link from a backwards one.

The change: a signal becomes a query, not a prompt

Once the four joins are in place, we stop asking a model what matters and start describing what matters, once, in a pattern the database can look for. Every pattern has the same shape:

WHEN   <this shape appears in the graph>
AND    <it touches something KSSL sells or could sell>
THEN   raise a signal, with the evidence that produced it

Six patterns cover what KSSL actually pays attention to. Nothing here is a prompt; each one is a query anybody can read, argue with and change.

Which ones go to the top

Every signal is scored the same way, and the score is arithmetic rather than opinion:

priority = severity  ×  confidence  ×  urgency  ×  relevance
severitythe typed number — contract value, quantity, size of the spec gap — or an agreed floor when nothing is stated
confidencethe weakest grade anywhere in the evidence chain: matched a label > matched an embedding > guessed
urgencyhow fast this kind of signal goes stale — a tender deadline decays in days, a spec gap in years
relevancedistance through the tree from the signal's node to KSSL's own portfolio nodes — computed, not asserted
Confidence is the weakest link in the chain, never the average. A guess that passes through three careful steps is still a guess, and averaging is exactly how a system launders one into a certainty.

What the model still does

It writes the sentence. Given a signal the graph has already found, and the statements that produced it, the model turns them into a line a person can read. That is a much smaller job than the one it does today, and it fails in a much cheaper way: today a bad output is an invented fact, and after this it is an awkward wording — because the facts came from the graph and carry the character offsets to prove it.

Step 1 — the ontology says these are the same kind of thing

An M777A2 Ultra-Light Howitzer and a Mobile Tactical Cannon share no words. Once J1 has typed them, both hang under materiel.weapons.artillery, and so does KSSL's own line — the corpus says so in its own words:

  • Kalyani Group —[has realised major breakthrough]→ in Artillery segment (indiandefencereview.com)
  • Kalyani Group —[develops]→ Artillery platforms (indiandefencereview.com)

Step 2 — the links become traversable

J2 turns three different phrasings into three legal link types, and checks each one against what may sit at either end:

procuresGovernment of India → M777A2 sustainment — Agent → Artefact, legal
replacesMobile Tactical Cannon → M777 towed artillery — Artefact → Artefact, legal
awardedHanwha Defense USA → US Army contract — Agent → Activity, legal

Step 3 — the pattern fires

Nobody wrote a prompt about M777 howitzers. Two of the six standing patterns simply match:

Rival advancea rival gains an awarded link into materiel.weapons.artillery — a node KSSL sells into
Open demanda buyer procures inside that same node, and there is no link from KSSL to that buyer

Step 4 — what KSSL is told

India is committing to keep the M777 flying while the platform's largest operator starts replacing it — and the replacement contract went to Hanwha, who is building US production and competes with us in India. Our ultralight artillery line sits on both sides of that: a sustainment and upgrade window opening now, and a replacement-class rival acquiring its reference customer.

And if anyone asks why we believe it, the answer is mechanical rather than a matter of trust: the signal names the pattern, the pattern names the links, the links name the statements, and each statement names the character range and the URL it came from. Every hop above is a row you can go and look at.

And what it got wrong — which is the useful part

Roughly a quarter of what fired is junk, and the junk is specific rather than random. Three failures, each naming the join that fixes it:

“the collaboration” supplies…the subject is a phrase, not a company — J4, which requires a name before a thing may be an actor
“weak enforcement replaces all protection”an Arabic article about policing and corruption; protection matched the armour node by spelling — J1, where meaning decides the type, not a substring
“perolehan 20 buah kenderaan perisai”a correct Malay armoured-vehicle signal with a sentence fragment where the buyer's name belongs — J4 again

None of those need a cleverer prompt. Each is exactly the work J1 and J4 already describe, which is the strongest evidence we have that the four joins are the right four.

Why this is better than what we have

Not a criticism of the current pipeline — it does the job it was built for. This is about what it structurally cannot do.

Today: a model reads one article After: a pattern reads the graph
What it readsOne article at a time. Nothing else is in scope.The whole graph, every article we have ever read, plus the ontology.
Who decides it mattersThe model, in one pass, with no memory of yesterday.A written pattern. The model never decides; it only writes the sentence.
Can it spot an absenceNo. An article cannot say “and KSSL was not invited”.Yes. A missing link is a queryable fact, and absence is often the signal.
Can it see a trendNo. A trend needs many documents and a clock.Yes. Counting links per quarter is a query.
When it is wrongIt invents a fact and we need a gate per field to catch it.It picks a wrong word. The facts came from the graph and cite offsets.
Why do you believe itBecause the model said so.signal → pattern → links → statements → character range → URL.
Cost per signalOne call to a 14 B model per document, forever, re-run on every change.Model work happens once at bind time; a signal is then a database query.

The line that matters most is the second-to-last. We have spent real time building a validation gate for every field a model writes, because the field without a gate is exactly where the invented facts collect. This approach removes most of the need for those gates by removing the opportunity: the model is no longer the thing that produces facts.

What could go wrong, and how we would know

Every one of these is something we can measure rather than argue about.

The worryHow it shows itself
The tree is wrong for a branch KSSL cares about.Residue per node is reported every run. A node that keeps sending things to unclassified is the tree telling us where it is thin. That is why the open slot exists and why nothing is force-fitted into the nearest node.
Typing is confidently wrong, and a wrong type is worse than none.Each binding carries how it was made — matched a label, matched an embedding, or set by a person. The signal's confidence is the weakest link in its own chain, so a guess can never be laundered into a certainty by passing through three steps.
We build the graph and the signals are boring.Testable before we build it. Take the six patterns, run them by hand over the corpus we already have, and count how many fire and how many a reader would act on. If that number is small, the patterns are wrong — and we have learned it in a day rather than a month.
Numbers get compared that should not be.The property registry declares a dimension and an applies_to for every property, and a comparison that crosses either is refused rather than rendered.
Something upstream changes and the page quietly goes stale.This page is generated. Every count in it is read from the database at build time, and the worked example is traced out of live rows — so it cannot be edited into agreement with a story that is no longer true.

The order to build it in

StepSize Why here
1Prove the patterns — done— Ran three of the six over the live corpus with no model at all. 39 signals, about three quarters of them real, and the failures name the joins that fix them. docs/prove_patterns.py.
2J4 — clean identity1 day A stop-list for boilerplate entities and a merge pass for the obvious spelling groups. Everything downstream counts things, so counting the right things comes first.
3J1 — type the entities2–3 days Labels first, embeddings second, open slot third. Report residue per language, because an English-only pass will look excellent and be broken.
4J2 — type the links2–3 days Lexical map reaches 18%; a small model picks one of 18 labels for the rest. The domain and range check runs on every link and drops the illegal ones.
5J3 — type the numbers2 days Dates, money, calibres, quantities. Unlocks value-at-stake, deadlines and spec tables.
6Signals as queries3 days Six patterns, the priority score, and the evidence chain behind every one.
Step 1 comes first on purpose. It costs a day and it is the only step that can tell us the other five are not worth doing.

Nothing here needs a bigger model. J2 asks a model to choose one of 33 labels and J1 asks it to choose one of 62 nodes — small, closed decisions that a small fast model makes as well as a large one, and far cheaper. The large model keeps only the writing.

A closed predicate list turns out to be a buying advantage. A survey of the 2026 extraction literature found that no released model does what our current extractor does — open-ended verbs, character offsets and 26 languages at once — and that every fast model on the market gives up the open verbs to get the speed. But J2 does not want open verbs. It wants exactly 33 labels, which is the shape those models are built for: there are 0.3 B encoders, Apache-licensed and roughly twenty times smaller than our judge, that emit a chosen relation label with the character offsets of both ends. The constraint the market treats as a limitation is our specification.

One caveat carried over from that survey, and it is the familiar one: every “multilingual” claim attached to those models is a claim about the encoder they were built on, not a measurement. The one family that does publish per-language numbers scores Chinese twenty points below Portuguese. We measure on Russian, Ukrainian, Turkish, Hebrew, Persian and Greek ourselves before we believe any of it.
